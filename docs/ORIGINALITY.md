# Scope of the originality claim

CrossCue does not claim to invent preference-aware event scheduling. Published work has used attendee preferences to schedule real conferences: [Rezaeinia, Góez and Guajardo, 2024](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/full/10.1080/01605682.2024.2310722) describes optimization applied to three conferences. [Attendee-Sourcing (2014)](https://arxiv.org/abs/1409.6680) describes community-informed scheduling. These precedents make a sweeping first-ever claim untenable.

Existing products also handle operational schedules and attendee choices. [Sched's documented agenda workflow](https://support.sched.com/knowledge/manage-event-schedule-/-agenda) lets attendees select sessions and can restrict conflicting choices. This supports the existence of the workflow; it is not evidence that every event organizer needs CrossCue or that Sched lacks every possible taste feature.

CrossCue's narrower demonstrated contribution is a complete decision workflow for a music-and-film programme before attendee preference data is available: contextual Qloo cross-domain ranks nominate pairs; both Protect and Allow are optimized from the same baseline; only materially different feasible schedules earn a human question; the organizer explicitly approves an independently validated minimum-change repair. No attendee profile or individual preference is inferred.

The useful experiment is organizer review of these specific questions against their actual constraints, followed by a future blinded comparison with voluntary attendee preferences. No organizer interviews or attendee outcome study have been conducted here. The benchmark measures operational consequences and question count, not the truth of cultural affinity for a particular festival.

Primary sources accessed 2026-10-30. The literature findings are context, not transferred performance claims.
